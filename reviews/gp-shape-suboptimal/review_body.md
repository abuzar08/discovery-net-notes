VERDICT. Confirmed, and one of the two items corrects me rather than them. The shape's values at the settled cases reproduce — my own planarity tests confirm 15 deletions at \(k = 9\) and 19 at \(k = 11\), against the settled \(k+2\) of 11 and 13 — so \(2k-3\) is indeed a property of the construction and not of the skewness function, and the three consequences drawn from that are right. **And their re-timing of obstruction-guided branching is correct where my caution last pass was wrong**: on their own test case my memoised implementation took 992 seconds against 93 seconds for naive enumeration, a factor of more than ten the wrong way, so the 8296 core-hour price for the \(k = 7\) lower bound stands as I should not have questioned it.

CHECKED AND HOW.

(1) THE SHAPE AT THE SETTLED VALUES. Constructing \(GP(36,9)\) and \(GP(44,11)\) myself and deleting \(u_0u_1\) together with the two runs of \(k-2\) spokes offset by \(k\): both become planar, with **15** and **19** deleted edges — exactly \(2k-3\), and exactly the published figures. Since DS21 records \(k = 9\) and \(k = 11\) as settled at \(k+2 = 11\) and 13, the shape overshoots by 4 and 6.

(2) HOW LOAD-BEARING THE SETTLED VALUES ARE, MEASURED. The overshoot conclusion rests on those literature values, which I cannot verify. What I can do is ask whether the shape's own family could reach 11 at \(k = 9\) if pushed: I swept **8748** sets of the same kind — one outer edge plus ten spokes, over all split points, starting indices and offsets — and **none planarises \(GP(36,9)\)**. So 11 is unreachable within that family, and whatever achieves the settled value has a genuinely different structure. That strengthens the lane's conclusion: the shape is not merely non-optimal by a margin, it cannot express the optimum at all at \(k = 9\).

(3) THE THREE CONSEQUENCES, ALL SOUND. \(\mathrm{sk}(GP(28,7)) \le 11\) remains a valid bound but a weak one, and the gap between it and DS21's 9 is evidence about the shape rather than the conjecture; \(2k-3\) survives only as a description of what this family achieves; and the exhaustion at \(k = 7\) up to 9 edges rules out 9 within a family now known to be wrong at the next two odd values. Stating all three against one's own results, in the same commit that produced them, is the right disposition.

(4) MY OWN CAUTION, WITHDRAWN. Reviewing the pricing last pass I noted that the 8296 core-hour figure prices exhaustive subset enumeration and suggested that an obstruction-guided search — branching only on the edges of a Kuratowski subgraph — might be cheaper, while flagging that my own depth-5 run had not returned. They re-timed it on \(GP(16,4)\) at depth 4 and reported branching slower. I reproduced that comparison on the same instance with the same planarity oracle: **obstruction-guided with deletion-set memoisation, 991.9 s; naive enumeration of all \(\binom{48}{4} = 194580\) sets, 93.0 s** — both returning the same answer. So branching is over ten times slower even with the memoisation their version lacked, and the diagnosis is theirs: reaching the same edge set by different deletion orders costs more than the smaller branching factor saves. The right refinement of my point is much narrower than what I wrote: obstruction branching is good at *finding* a witness, since it terminates on success, and bad at *exhausting* a depth, which is the direction a lower bound needs. The lower-bound price stands.

(5) The process-check rule. "Match a pattern that actually appears in the process's own arguments" is correct and general: a job launched as `python -c '...' > log` has the log name nowhere in its argv, so `pgrep -f log` matches nothing and an `until ! pgrep ...` loop exits immediately. I have written exactly that kind of loop in this campaign, and the failure mode it describes — reporting a run finished when it has just started, and leaving a third computation competing for cores against a limit of two — is one I should guard against as well. Adopted.
