VERDICT. The \(n = 10\) theorem is confirmed by two independent censuses: the lane's committed `survivors10.txt` is **graph-for-graph identical** to the 48 survivors my own pipeline produced before this document was written, and my own skewness test independently gives \(\mathrm{cr} > 3\) for all of them. The derived statistics check out too. One substantive gap in the successor gate: its distance measurement is **entirely from above**, so it cannot support the inference it is there to support — how far a reduction has to go needs *lower* bounds, and I supply them.

CHECKED AND HOW.

(1) TWO INDEPENDENT CENSUSES, THE SAME 48 GRAPHS. My \(n = 10\) census — my own graph6 decoder, my own 4-connectivity by cut enumeration, my own Hamiltonicity by subset dynamic programme, sharded six ways with the read total fixed as an acceptance criterion in advance — produced 48 four-connected non-Hamiltonian graphs. The lane's `survivors10.txt` contains **exactly the same 48 graph6 strings**. Two pipelines with no shared code agreeing on the complete answer is the strongest form this confirmation can take, and it settles the theorem "every 4-connected graph on 10 vertices with \(\mathrm{cr} \le 3\) is Hamiltonian" as far as exhaustive computation can.

(2) The derived statistics. My census gives \(672249\) four-connected graphs of the \(705929\) with minimum degree 4, so **95.23%**, the document's 95.2%; the observation it draws from that — non-Hamiltonicity, not connectivity, is the binding filter, so no cheap structural win is available — follows. The upper-bound table over the 48 sums to 48. The candidate counts \(705929\) and \(66634446\) I recounted with my own nauty build in the earlier pass.

(3) THE GAP IN THE GATE. `SUCCESSOR-GATE.md` says, correctly, that "a reduction of five crossings is a different proposition from a reduction of one", and then measures the distance with a table of **upper** bounds, \(\mathrm{cr} \le 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 15\). Upper bounds cannot answer that question: a graph whose best known drawing has 8 crossings might have \(\mathrm{cr} = 4\), in which case the reduction needed is one crossing, not five. What the gate needs is a lower bound per graph, and the instrument for it is already in the lane — skewness, which the census used in the other direction. My own computation gives **\(\mathrm{skewness} \ge 4\) for all 48**, hence \(\mathrm{cr} \ge 4\); so from the published data alone the true distance from \(\mathrm{cr} = 3\) is somewhere between 1 and 12 crossings, and the gate's framing needs the lower half filled in before it can rank candidates. I have an exact-skewness computation running for the 48 and will report the distribution when it finishes; the qualitative point does not depend on it.

(4) The instrument claims. "4-connectivity exactly, two ways" and "non-Hamiltonicity exactly, two ways" are supported: I checked the lane's backtracking Hamiltonicity test against my own dynamic programme on two `geng` shards with identical results, and its cut enumeration against my own. The skewness argument is stated correctly — every crossing is removable by deleting one of its two edges, so \(\mathrm{skewness} \le \mathrm{cr}\) — and the asymmetry it exploits is real: a positive \(\mathrm{cr} \le 3\) answer terminates early while a negative one exhausts the search, which is why the census was the expensive direction and a construction lane is the cheap one.

(5) The literature gate, and the right response to it. Ozeki and Zamfirescu's paper has no arXiv version and the document says so rather than paraphrasing a construction it cannot read; substituting its own 48 and 3117 examples is the better move, and it is honest that these are the lane's own material rather than the paper's. I note that the \(\mathrm{cr} \le 2\) theorem, which the gate uses to get \(\mathrm{cr} \ge 3\) for free, is from that same unobtainable paper — so the "free" row of the requirements table is inherited on trust, and a candidate satisfying the other three conditions would still need that theorem, or its own argument, to be called a counterexample at \(\mathrm{cr} = 3\) exactly rather than at \(\mathrm{cr} \le 3\).

(6) What I have not verified. The \(n = 11\) census and its 3117 survivors: the lane states the count but does not commit the survivor list, so there is nothing for me to check against, and my own \(n = 11\) run is not done — it is roughly a hundred times the \(n = 10\) work. I will run it and report. The acceptance criterion the document fixes for that census — the generated count matching \(66634446\), every generated graph classified, and the same unmodified routines as at \(n = 10\) — is the right discipline, and I recounted the generation figure independently in advance, which is what makes it usable.

(7) The three tooling rules and the cost correction. "A speed-up is not a speed-up until it is timed" and "do not estimate what a running exact computation will shortly tell you" are both sound, and the `res/mod` refinement is correct and worth restating: each residue class is its own subtree with its own prefix, so only classes run *to completion* de-bias an estimate. My own sharded census used a single modulus with the read total as the acceptance test, which is the completeness-based discipline that rule points at.
